## Part 1: Story Thus Far

Dzikri’s story starts in a place where safety never really settled into the body. He grew up in an environment where bullying came from school, from brothers, and from parents who were abusive, so the first lesson his nervous system learned was not trust, but survive. And here’s what’s really happening beneath all the self criticism he’s carrying now: when a child grows up in chaos, he doesn’t just learn that people can hurt him, he also learns that his own voice probably won’t protect him. So he adapts. He gets quiet. He smiles through things. He becomes the one who can take it. That sounds like resilience, and it is, but it also has a cost. If you spend years making it through by staying agreeable, staying small, and not causing trouble, you eventually become an adult who can read other people very well while struggling to hear himself clearly.

That’s why his relationship with money is so tangled. As a child, money was inconsistent in the most confusing way. At first, his father provided everything, so money felt like something that would appear when needed. He didn’t have to build discipline around it, earn it, or understand it. Then the divorce hit, the support disappeared, and suddenly there was hunger, embarrassment, and dependency. That kind of swing does something deep. It creates a mind that sees money as both freedom and fragility. You can see that playing out now in the way he talks about spending, saving, loans, and the urge to get rich quickly. Part of him still believes money should come in a way that’s effortless, because that was the childhood model. Another part knows that’s not true, and that tension makes him oscillate between urgency and avoidance. He wants wealth, but he doesn’t yet fully trust the process that creates it.

The same pattern shows up in his work history. He has real capability. That part is not in question. He learned videography, editing, planning, production, social media work, and even now he’s experimenting with AI and website building. He’s not lazy in the basic sense. He can work hard when there’s a clear deadline, a visible consequence, or a direct need. The proof is right there in the way he paid off loans fast once something clicked, and in the way he can pull down good money when the structure is obvious. The real issue is that he doesn’t sustain effort well when the payoff feels distant, unclear, or dependent on people he doesn’t trust. The reason this pattern developed is simple: as a kid, effort didn’t reliably lead to safety, respect, or reward. In fact, he was often punished or overlooked no matter what he did. So now, when progress isn’t immediate, his system reads it as danger or pointless pain, and he starts reaching for escape. That’s not a character flaw as much as an old survival reflex.

His business experiences made this worse, but they also revealed something important. He kept ending up around people who promised big outcomes and delivered very little. That wasn’t random. People with his history often get drawn to charismatic but unstable environments because they’re familiar. He was already used to being dependent on people who didn’t protect him, so he could tolerate unfairness longer than most. He kept hoping the next mentor, the next friend, the next team would finally be the one that turns everything around. And when that didn’t happen, the disappointment didn’t just feel like a bad business deal. It confirmed an old fear that trusting people is dangerous and maybe even foolish. That’s why his trust issues aren’t just about those friends. They’re about a lifetime of being let down by the people who were supposed to model care, fairness, and direction.

What’s striking is that he has developed a strong moral compass out of all this pain. He hates unfairness because he’s lived it. He wants to treat future employees fairly because he knows what it feels like to be undervalued. He wants to build a home where people feel safe because he remembers what it was like to not have one. He wants his future wife to not work because part of him is trying to repair the helplessness he felt watching women in his family and himself carry burdens without real protection. His dream of the bungalow, the cars, the happy family, the people gathered around him, isn’t just greed. It’s proof. It’s his way of saying, “I came from nothing stable, and I can create something secure and impressive.” Underneath the flashy image is a wounded boy who wants dignity.

But his self image hasn’t caught up with his actual capacity. That’s the core conflict. He knows he can do things, but he doesn’t feel like the kind of person who can consistently do them. He has evidence that he can earn, pay off debt, learn new skills, and adapt. Yet inside, he still talks like someone who expects to fail halfway through. That gap between proven ability and internal worth is where most of his suffering lives. He’s not lacking talent. He’s lacking the inner experience of being sustained, guided, and reliably rewarded. So he tries to compensate with perfectionism, big fantasies, or quitting before the disappointment gets too deep. If he stops early, then maybe it wasn’t a full failure. If he doesn’t fully commit, then maybe it doesn’t count as another wound.

His relationships reflect that same push and pull. He wants closeness, but he keeps distance from family because the old hurt is still alive. He has a girlfriend and clearly wants to provide, protect, and create ease for her, which shows tenderness and responsibility, but even that can become a way of proving his worth rather than simply living it. With friends and business partners, he’s cautious, skeptical, and often silent until frustration boils over. He doesn’t lack love. He lacks safety. And because safety was never stable growing up, he now tries to build it through money, status, and control.

So who is Dzikri at his core? He’s not the lazy man he fears he is. He’s a hurt, intelligent, ambitious person with a strong hunger for dignity, but an underdeveloped sense of self-trust. He’s learned how to survive, how to spot unfairness, how to work when necessary, and how to keep going even when disappointed. But he still carries the old belief that unless something is clearly visible and immediately rewarding, it probably won’t work out. That belief has protected him from more pain, but it’s also kept him stuck. The foundation he’s standing on right now is not failure. It’s a nervous system trained by chaos, a mind trained by broken promises, and a heart that still wants a life bigger than the one he was handed. That’s the truth underneath all the doubt.

## Part 2: Current Self

*[Photo: Childhood Home #1]*

I grew up in a home that did not feel safe or steady. There was abuse, bullying, and a lot of hurt, and even as a kid I learned to smile through it. At home and at school, I was often made to feel small, so I started doubting my own voice before I even knew what it was. Money was confusing too. For a while, my father could provide anything I asked for, so I never learned the value of earning or saving. After the divorce, that comfort disappeared and I had to deal with hunger, uncertainty, and a life where I often went to school without eating. That taught me how easy it is to drift, but it also showed me why I now crave security, stability, and a home that feels warm instead of heavy. I still carry the effects of that childhood, especially the habit of taking the easy way out, but I also know those early years gave me a deep hunger to build something better.

*[Photo: Your Turning Point — Income Slip]*

One of the biggest turning points in my life was realizing that I could survive on my own when things got hard. There were times I had no choice but to go out and find work, and I discovered that I can move fast when I have a clear need in front of me. A recent example was paying off my overdue loans after months of delay. Once something finally clicked, I went back to work properly and cleared it in about a month and a half. That mattered to me because it proved I am not useless, even if I doubt myself a lot. I also learned hard lessons from people I trusted, especially when I stayed in work that paid me very little for years and gave me almost nothing in return. Those experiences taught me to value fairness, honesty, and clear expectations. I do not want to repeat pain for the sake of hope. I want to build with eyes open.

My path started in film, but a friend pulled me into social media videography, and that shift taught me a lot about shooting, editing, planning, and how fast the digital world moves. I learned by doing, even when I felt undervalued or unsure of myself. Later I moved through different business settings, met a lot of owners, and saw how many of them looked successful from the outside while struggling underneath. That made me more cautious and more aware of what I do not want. More recently, I stepped away from business work that was draining me and went back to earning through jobs and freelance-style work, including video and AI-based projects. I still notice the same pattern in myself: I do best when the goal is clear and close, but I stall when the outcome feels distant. Even so, this whole journey has given me a practical eye, a strong work ethic when I choose to use it, and a real understanding of what fair work should look like. That is exactly the kind of foundation I will need for what comes next.

*[Photos: Current Friend Group | Family Group]*

Right now, I live alone in an apartment in Singapore and spend most of my time working to cover bills and buy the things I want. I am also starting to save, which still feels a little surprising to me. My days are simple and very repetitive: I wake up, spend a bit of time on Instagram, shower, then head to work. I usually schedule my day in blocks so I will actually show up, because I know I can be lazy if I leave too much freedom. After work, I come home, eat, and either go back out for another shift, work on AI ideas, or end up watching YouTube until I sleep. I have a girlfriend, and I like being able to buy things for her and make her comfortable. I am not living with my family, and even though I can talk to them about work, I keep my personal life at a distance. We are closer now than we used to be, and there is more laughter when we meet, but I still do not open up easily. 

At work, I am more stable than before, but I do not feel fully satisfied. I can earn around three thousand a month when I stay active, yet if I stop working, everything drops fast. That keeps me anxious about money, about becoming ordinary, and about not reaching the life I keep imagining. My strengths are that I learn fast, I can set a goal and hit it when it feels real, and I am still curious enough to keep trying new tools like AI. My weaknesses are also clear to me: I self-doubt, I avoid hard conversations, I procrastinate, and I often choose the easier path when the bigger one feels far away. Even with that, I am trying to build a steadier life now, one where I do not have to keep starting over.

## Part 3: Inspirations & Dream Life

What I admire most is not just skill, but truth and confidence. Alex Homose [Hormozi] stands out because he feels genuine, and he shares things that actually work. That tells me I value real usefulness over empty hype. Andrew Tate, for me, represents a different kind of lesson: intense confidence, certainty, and the kind of self-belief that makes a man stand taller. I see that these two inspirations point to the same thing in me. I want to be someone grounded enough to learn what is real, and strong enough to own my presence without hesitation. I am drawn to people who move with clarity, speak with conviction, and create results. That is not random admiration. It is a reflection of the man I am building toward: practical, confident, and impossible to ignore.

*[Photos: Dream Home #1 | Dream Home #2]*

My dream home is not about showing off. It is about freedom, space, and being able to take care of the people I love. I can see myself in a home that feels open, calm, and worthy of the life I have built, whether that is in New Zealand with mountains around me or in Dubai with a life that feels bigger than the one I have now. This home represents the three goals that matter most to me: providing for my family, reaching real financial freedom, and creating a life I do not need to escape from. To me, financial freedom means never worrying about bills, being able to support my family properly, and choosing my days instead of reacting to them. I want a home that proves I made it to a safer, stronger place.

*[Photos: Dream Car #1 (Audi R8) | Dream Car #2 (Aston Martin Vanquish) | Dream Car #3 (Pagani Huayra Roadster)]*

My dream car says a lot about how I want to feel when I move through the world. I am drawn to an Audi R8, a BMW Z4, and if life gets that big, a Pagani Huayra Roadster or Aston Martin Vanquish because they feel sharp, powerful, and beautiful without trying too hard. That is what I want my life to feel like too. When I drive a car like that, it is not just about speed. It is about confidence, control, and enjoying the reward of hard work. I want to sit inside and feel proud before I even start the engine. I want the sound, the design, the interior, and the way it handles to remind me that I built something real. That car would feel like a symbol of trust in myself.

*[Photo: Dream Fashion #1]*

My dream fashion style is simple, and that tells me a lot. I do not need loud clothes to feel like myself. A clean plain shirt and plain pants are enough when I am living with confidence. I want my style to feel effortless, neat, and calm, the kind of look that says I know who I am without needing to prove it. That same energy is what I want in the places I travel. I imagine New Zealand with wide green landscapes and mountains, and Switzerland with fresh air, open views, and peaceful surroundings. I want my clothes to fit that life naturally, simple enough to move freely, polished enough to feel put together anywhere I go. My style and my travel dreams both point to the same image: a man who is relaxed, clear, and at home anywhere beautiful.

*[Photo: Dream Fitness #1]*

My dream fitness level is not about looking impressive for other people. It is about having the energy to live the life I want without feeling lazy, weak, or held back. If I am fit, I will feel more confident in everything I do, and that changes my whole day. I want to wake up with coffee or tea, work for a short period, then come home and spend real time with my family. I want dinner with them to feel peaceful, not rushed. After that, I want the option to swim, go to the gym, or one day even drive fast on a track. My body should support my life, not fight it. That is what fitness means to me: steady energy, confidence, freedom to travel, and the strength to enjoy quiet family nights and big adventures with the same ease.

## Part 4: Future Self

Right now, money is taking up too much space in my head because it feels like the key to everything. I want to move, breathe, help people, pay my bills, and not feel stuck every time I look at my life. I am working, so things are a little better than before, but I still feel that tension inside me. I know I can do more than just get by. I know I want to build a life where I can take care of myself and also show up for my girlfriend and anyone who needs me. What I feel most is this strange mix of relief and frustration. Relief because I have some income. Frustration because I know I keep standing at the edge of the life I want instead of fully stepping into it.

My future self is calm about money because he knows how to make it without chasing it like a desperate man. He works with purpose, not panic. He still cares about money, but it no longer controls his mood or decisions. He has confidence that comes from proof, not wishful thinking. He does not waste all day overthinking ideas. He picks one thing, stays with it, and turns his knowledge into something useful. He knows how to lead himself first, and because of that, other people trust him too. He is disciplined, patient, and hard to shake. He does not confuse being busy with being productive. He builds things that last because he knows how to keep going after the excitement is gone. He has a growth mindset that is not just a nice phrase, but something visible in how he handles pressure, failure, and setbacks. He is not perfect, but he is reliable. He finishes what he starts. He believes in himself enough to keep showing up when nobody is clapping yet.

What is holding me back is not a lack of ideas. I have already proven I can think of business, content, coffee, and different ways to make things happen. The real problem is me giving up too early. I start with hope, then I hit silence, slow progress, or discomfort, and I back off. I let laziness wear the mask of waiting for the right moment. I let overthinking pretend it is planning. I keep telling myself I need more motivation, when the truth is I need more discipline. I say I want money, but sometimes I do not stay in the work long enough to deserve it. That is the conflict. I want the life of a man who builds, but I still act like a man who quits when it gets boring, hard, or unclear. I do not need another dream. I need to stop abandoning the ones I already have. If I stay loyal to my excuses, I will keep repeating the same year over and over.

The one thing that must change is my discipline. That is the bridge. Not a better idea, not more motivation, not another fresh start. I need to become the kind of man who keeps going when progress is slow and nobody is rewarding him yet. If I do that, everything else starts moving. If I do not, I already know what the regret will feel like. Three or five years from now, I will be sick of looking back at the same pattern, the same half-built plans, the same excuses, and the same feeling that I almost became who I wanted to be. I do not want to keep visiting my future through disappointment. I want to build it. And that starts now, by staying with the work instead of walking away from it.
